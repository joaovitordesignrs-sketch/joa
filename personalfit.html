Personal Fit

Rebuilding user trust and reducing churn from 30% to less than 4% per month.

01 — Context

A startup, zero references, and total ownership

When I joined Personal Fit, I had just been promoted to mid-level. And then they threw me into a startup taking its first steps, as the sole product designer. No internal references, nobody to review decisions, no established process. It was scary and liberating at the same time.

This case is about a problem I took a while to name correctly. At first it seemed like a bug. Then it seemed like a communication problem. Only when I actually cross-referenced the data did it become clear what was happening.

02 — The Problem

30% churn, 1-star ratings, and a product perceived as broken

The app had 30% monthly churn. Play Store and App Store ratings regularly hit 1 star. And the most frustrating part: the product had already gone through a complete refactor. New engineering, new design system, new flows. We'd done our homework — but the numbers wouldn't improve.

The internal narrative was "there are still bugs." And fine, bugs exist. But something didn't add up.

The insight that changed the entire approach

03 — Discovery

What the data was actually saying

In a cohort analysis, I cross-referenced new user behavior with old user behavior. And a pattern emerged that didn't make sense at first: new users had a significantly better experience than old users — even using the exact same refactored product.

What I realized was that old users had been so exposed to real failures in the past that they'd developed a kind of conditioned distrust. Any friction — even intentional, even correct — was interpreted as a bug. They no longer trusted the product. And this distrust was so deeply rooted that even a completely new product couldn't automatically undo it.

This has a name in psychology: learned helplessness, originally documented by Martin Seligman in 1967. The idea is simple: when someone is repeatedly exposed to situations they can't control, they develop the expectation that things will keep going wrong — even when the environment changes.

In practice, the product violated Nielsen's Heuristic #1 — Visibility of System Status: there was no clear signal that this was a renewed product. For the old user, it was the same app with the same history of problems.

New Users

Positive experience, higher retention, fewer complaints. Evaluated the product based on its current state.

Old Users

Negative perception persisted despite fixes. Any friction was interpreted as a bug from past conditioning.

"If satisfied users existed but simply weren't rating — and dissatisfied users were actively rating — then the public perception problem was partly a volume problem. We needed to activate the voices that were silent."

The strategic hypothesis

05 — Solution

A review funnel with two exits

I designed a three-step strategy, simple in structure but careful in logic.

  • Task-based activation — the user completed a task inside the app before any review prompt. This wasn't random. The Peak-End Rule (Kahneman & Tversky) says people evaluate an experience mainly by its peak and end — not the average. Positioning the review prompt right after a positive moment significantly increased the chance of a favorable response.
  • Simplified in-app rating — a 1-to-5 star rating directly in the app, no immediate store redirect. Less friction, more honesty.
  • Sentiment bifurcation — scores of 4-5 were invited to publish that review on the store. Voluntary, no pressure. Scores of 1-3 entered a qualitative question flow instead. We turned frustration into data — and prevented it from becoming a public review.
06 — Results

What happened after

Today, in 2026, the app has churn below 4% per month and consistently high store ratings. The same engineering and interface designed in 2022 remain in production. The user base grew.

07 — Learning

Metrics rarely point to the root cause

What I learned from this project was that product metrics rarely point to the root cause. 30% churn looked like a product problem. It was a memory problem — the memory the user had of the product before, overlaid onto the product that existed now.

And that sometimes the most effective solution isn't changing the product, but changing how it's perceived.

"Sometimes the most effective solution isn't changing the product, but changing how it's perceived."
